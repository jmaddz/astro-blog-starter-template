This December, eighteen years after my first professional massage course in Aotearoa
New Zealand, I graduate with a Bachelor of Health Science (Clinical Myotherapy) from
Torrens University. It was a part-time degree, done over six years while working
full-time in practice. I want to tell you why I did it, because the "why" explains a
lot about how I work.

## The short version of a long road

I started as a Remedial Massage Therapist in 2007, moved to Melbourne in 2012, and
spent most of those years treating the same things again and again: backs, necks,
shoulders, headaches, the recurring niggles that bring people back monthly. Hands-on
work helped people, genuinely. But I kept meeting presentations where I could feel
that something was driving the pain from somewhere I hadn't fully assessed.

So I went back to school. Anatomy I knew well enough to teach it (I tutored surface
anatomy and musculoskeletal anatomy at the New Zealand College of Massage in 2018),
but the degree added the structured layers I was missing: orthopaedic and
neurological assessment, pain science, joint mobilisation, dry needling and
electrical dry needling, exercise rehabilitation, and how to measure whether a
treatment plan is actually working.

## What changes, and what doesn't

What changes is the diagnostic process. An appointment now starts with a proper
assessment, not with hands on the sore spot. What doesn't change is my hands, my
pace, and the way I work with your nervous system, which was always the real target
tissue anyway.

If you're curious what Clinical Myotherapy is, I've written
[a plain-language explainer](/what-is-myotherapy). If you've been seeing me for years
as a remedial client, nothing about our sessions needs to change unless you want it
to. And if something persistent has been hanging around longer than it should, this
is exactly the kind of thing the new training is for.
