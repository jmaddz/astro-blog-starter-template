One of the most useful things I do in a session doesn't involve my hands at
all. It is a conversation, and it usually starts with a version of this:

**Pain is data, not a direct readout of tissue damage.**

## The strange fact about pain

Pain is produced by your nervous system, not by your tissues. It is a
protective output, a bit like a smoke alarm: calibrated to be over-sensitive,
because evolution has learned that a false alarm is cheap and a missed fire is
catastrophic. Usually that sensitivity is well-matched to actual tissue
danger. But sometimes the alarm stays loud long after the fire is out, or gets
triggered by burnt toast. Think of how much a paper cut hurts, versus how much
damage a tattoo involves. Pain intensity and tissue damage are related, but
they are not the same thing.

This is why "your scan looks terrible" and "your scan is fine" both tell you so
little. Plenty of people with ugly scans have no pain, and plenty of people
with clean scans hurt a lot. The scan is one input. Your nervous system is the
thing producing the experience, and it is listening to everything: the tissues,
yes, but also your sleep, your stress, your beliefs about what's wrong, and
whether moving feels safe.

## What this means in a session

When I say pain is data, I mean it is information worth taking seriously,
without automatically treating it as proof of harm. In practice, that
conversation changes what we do next. It changes how hard we push, how quickly
we progress, and whether "hurts a bit while doing it" means "stop" or "safe to
continue". Understanding that distinction, between hurt and harm, is often the
moment a persistent problem starts to unwind.

## Education is treatment

And I mean that literally, not as a nice idea. Pain neuroscience education,
the work of Moseley, Butler, and Farmer, measurably reduces pain and disability
when delivered well. I suspect that's partly because a nervous system that
understands itself is less frightened, and a less frightened nervous system
spends less energy sounding the alarm. When you leave my room, I want you to
know what happened, what it means, and what to do next. That is not the garnish
on the treatment. It is part of the treatment.
