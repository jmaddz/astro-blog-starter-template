One of the most useful things I do in a session doesn't involve my hands at all. It
is a conversation, and it usually starts with a version of this: **pain is data, not
a direct readout of damage.**

## The strange fact about pain

Pain is produced by your nervous system, not by your tissues. It is a protective
output, a signal that your brain has decided something needs guarding. Usually that
decision is well-calibrated to actual tissue danger. But sometimes the system stays
loud long after tissues have healed, or gets triggered by things that are not
threatening at all. Think of how much a paper cut hurts, versus how much damage a
tattoo involves. Pain intensity and tissue damage are related, but they are not the
same thing.

This is why "your scan looks terrible" and "your scan is fine" both tell you so
little. Plenty of people with ugly scans have no pain, and plenty of people with
clean scans hurt a lot. The scan is one input. Your nervous system is the thing
producing the experience.

## What this means in a session

When I say pain is data, I mean it is information worth taking seriously, without
automatically treating it as proof of harm. In practice, that conversation changes
what we do next. It changes how hard we push, how quickly we progress, and whether
"hurts a bit while doing it" means "stop" or "safe to continue". Understanding that
distinction, between hurt and harm, is often the moment a persistent problem starts
to unwind.

## Education is treatment

There is proper evidence for this. Pain neuroscience education, the work of Moseley,
Butler, and Farmer, measurably reduces pain and disability when delivered well,
because a nervous system that understands itself is less protective than one that is
frightened. When you leave my room, I want you to know what happened, what it means,
and what to do next. That is not the garnish on the treatment. It is part of the
treatment.
